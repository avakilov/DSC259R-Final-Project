Missingness Analysis

We examined missing values in the dataset to understand whether missing data occurred randomly or was associated with other variables. Several columns contained missing values, including OUTAGE.DURATION, DEMAND.LOSS.MW, and CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED, with DEMAND.LOSS.MW having the largest amount of missing data.

  • OUTAGE.DURATION – 58 missing values
  • DEMAND.LOSS.MW – 705 missing values
  • CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED – 443 missing values

Outage Duration by DEMAND.LOSS.MW Missingness

The distributions of outage duration look similar whether DEMAND.LOSS.MW is missing or present. A permutation test confirmed that missingness does not depend on outage duration (p = 0.6824).

Cause Category by DEMAND.LOSS.MW Missingness

However, missingness in DEMAND.LOSS.MW varies across outage causes. A permutation test showed strong evidence that missingness depends on CAUSE.CATEGORY (p < 0.001), suggesting that missing data is related to outage type rather than outage duration.

Hypothesis Testing

Following our exploratory analysis, we formally tested whether outages caused by severe weather tend to last longer than outages caused by other factors.

Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis (H₀): The mean outage duration for severe weather outages is equal to the mean outage duration for all other causes.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): The mean outage duration for severe weather outages is greater than the mean outage duration for other causes.

Test Statistic

Difference in mean outage duration:
T = mean(Durationsevere) − mean(Durationother)

Method

  • Performed a permutation test with 10,000 permutations
  • Randomly shuffled outage durations across all rows
  • Recomputed the difference in means for each shuffle
  • Constructed a null distribution of permuted differences
  • Calculated the p-value as the proportion of permuted statistics ≥ observed statistic

Results

Observed Difference

≈ 2,538 minutes

P-value

< 0.001

The observed difference indicates that severe weather outages last, on average, approximately 42 hours longer than outages caused by other factors. Since the p-value is less than 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis.

There is strong statistical evidence that outage duration depends on cause category. This result supports the importance of weather-related variables and motivates the use of machine learning models for predicting outage severity.

XGBoost Model

We trained an XGBoost Regressor as an additional boosting-based model to compare with the Random Forest baseline. XGBoost builds decision trees sequentially, allowing each new tree to correct the errors of previous trees. This approach can capture complex nonlinear relationships in the data and often improves predictive performance for tabular datasets.

  • Train/Test Split: 80% / 20%
  • Evaluation Metrics: MAE, RMSE, and R²
  • Feature Engineering: log-transform + standardization

XGBoost

R² ≈ 0.83
RMSE ≈ 1.19
MAE ≈ 0.67

LightGBM Model (Final Model)

To further improve performance, we trained a LightGBM Regressor, a gradient boosting framework optimized for efficiency and high performance on tabular datasets. LightGBM uses a leaf-wise tree growth strategy and histogram-based learning, allowing it to capture complex patterns in the data while remaining computationally efficient.

  • Train/Test Split: 80% / 20%
  • Evaluation Metrics: MAE, RMSE, and R²
  • Feature Engineering: log-transform + standardization

Among all models tested, LightGBM achieved the strongest predictive performance, explaining nearly all variation in the log-transformed severity measure.

We compared three tree-based models for predicting outage severity: Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. While the Random Forest provided a strong baseline, gradient boosting models captured more complex relationships in the data. LightGBM achieved the best performance, with the highest R² and lowest error metrics, and was selected as the final model for predicting outage severity.

Our baseline model is a Random Forest Regressor. This ensemble model captures nonlinear relationships between economic, climate, and demographic variables and outage severity.
